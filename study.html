AI search assistants — ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Google's AI Overviews — can only cite what their crawlers can read. So I checked what the web's biggest sites actually allow. One afternoon, three tiny HTTP requests per domain, every result reproducible.

Finding 1: One in five top sites blocks at least one AI crawler

Of 327 reachable sites in the Tranco top-500, 65 (20%) block at least one of the five major AI crawlers via robots.txt, and 35 (11%) block all five: Facebook, TikTok, Amazon, Reddit, the New York Times, the BBC, Reuters, Bloomberg, the WSJ — a wall of social networks, e-commerce giants, and news publishers. If you're wondering why AI assistants answer questions about products and news mostly from secondary sources — this is why.

The gradient is small but consistent: the crawler most associated with training data (CCBot) is blocked most; the one most associated with search citations (PerplexityBot) is blocked least. Sites are drawing a line between "cite me" and "train on me" — with robots.txt as the only pen available.

Finding 3: 8% block at the edge — where robots.txt audits can't see

26 sites (8%) serve their homepage normally to a browser user-agent but return 403 to a GPTBot user-agent. That's edge-level blocking — a Cloudflare "Block AI Bots" toggle or a WAF rule — happening before robots.txt is ever consulted. A site can have a perfectly permissive robots.txt and still be completely invisible to AI crawlers. If you've only audited your robots.txt, you haven't audited your AI visibility.

Finding 4: llms.txt is quietly becoming real

11% of the top 500 (37 sites) publish a spec-conformant llms.txt — including Adobe, Microsoft (azure.com), Cloudflare, GitHub, NVIDIA, Samsung, Stripe, Shopify, and Slack. For a convention that didn't exist not long ago, one in nine of the web's biggest properties is a real adoption number — and it's dominated by exactly the companies whose developer-facing content benefits most from being AI-legible.

Data-quality note worth stealing for your own audits: a naive "does /llms.txt return 200" check found 79 "adopters" — but 29 of those were SPA soft-404s returning an HTML shell for any URL, and 13 more served plain-text error pages. Only content validation (is it actually markdown with an H1?) gets you the honest 37. Most quick-check tools will overcount.

Methodology

Domain list: Tranco top-500 (the academic-standard ranking; snapshot July 13, 2026). Unreachable domains — DNS infrastructure, CDN endpoints, and sites that didn't answer — are excluded from the denominator (327 remained). Per domain: robots.txt parsed with Python's standard RobotFileParser for each of the five crawlers; homepage fetched twice (browser UA vs GPTBot UA) to detect edge blocking; llms.txt fetched and content-validated. Read-only, ~3 small requests per domain, politely rate-limited.

Every single-domain result is reproducible with the same code this study ran: ai-crawler-check, a free, dependency-free Python CLI — source on GitHub. Raw per-domain data available on request.

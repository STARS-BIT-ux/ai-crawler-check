Most AI-crawler blocking doesn't happen in robots.txt

I checked the Tranco top 2,000 domains, of which 1,530 serve a reachable robots.txt. Two thirds as many of them block AI crawlers at the network edge as declare a block in robots.txt — and they are largely not the same sites.

Declared · robots.txt

31120.3%

Disallow at least one of the five major AI crawlers in a file anyone can read.

Observed · HTTP edge

20513.4%

Return 401/403 to a declared AI user-agent while their robots.txt permits it.

n = 1,530 responding hosts, drawn from Tranco ranks 1–2,000 · 470 did not serve HTTP · fetched August 2026

Every tool I know of that answers "is my site open to AI crawlers?" answers it by parsing robots.txt. That is one layer. The request itself is another, and the two disagree often enough that reading only the first gives you the wrong answer for roughly one site in seven.

The ordering is stable and unsurprising: the crawler associated with open training corpora is blocked most, the one associated with an answer engine that sends referral traffic is blocked least. What is surprising is how small all five numbers are. Almost eighty percent of the largest sites on the web that serve a robots.txt still declare no restriction at all.

The divergence, in names you can check

These domains permit AI crawlers in robots.txt and then refuse the request. Thirty seconds with curl -A reproduces it:

  • pinterest.com
  • chatgpt.com
  • cloudflare.net
  • reddit.com
  • unity3d.com

I cannot see inside anyone's WAF, so I report this as suspected edge blocking. The observable fact is the status-code mismatch between the two layers, not its cause. A managed rule, a rate limiter and a deliberate policy all look identical from outside.

The data

All 2,000 ranked hosts, one row each, including the 470 excluded from the percentages — so you can redo the arithmetic on a different base if you disagree with mine.

Method

StepWhat was done
SampleTranco ranks 1–2,000, one contact per host
DeclaredFetch /robots.txt, parse rules for each of the five user-agents
ObservedRequest the homepage with each crawler's documented UA; record HTTP status
llms.txtFetch /llms.txt, require a parseable body, not merely a 200
Denominator1,530 of the 2,000 ranked hosts served a reachable robots.txt; the other 470 (DNS failure, or infrastructure domains that run no web server) are excluded from every percentage

The denominator is stated everywhere on purpose. An earlier, smaller pilot of this census got quoted back to me as both 13% and 20% by readers who assumed different bases.

What this does not show

Nothing legal. There is recent German case law on machine-readable opt-outs — Kneschke v. LAION, OLG Hamburg, 10 December 2025 — but it turned on plain-language versus machine-readable reservations, not on CDN behaviour. Extending it to edge configuration would be my inference, not the court's, so I don't make it.

Nothing about llms.txt being useful. 12.3% adoption is a fact about publishers, not about consumers. Whether any crawler reads the file is a separate question, and the available evidence is not encouraging.

Nothing about intent. A site that blocks at the edge may be doing it deliberately, or may have inherited a managed rule it has never seen. This measures behaviour, not policy.

Check your own domain

The checker is a single zero-dependency Python file in the repository:

python ai_crawler_check.py yourdomain.com

It runs both layers — the robots.txt parse and the user-agent request — and prints where they disagree.
